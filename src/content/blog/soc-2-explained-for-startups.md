A larger prospect is ready to buy, and then its security team asks for your SOC 2 report. If you don't have one, the worst move is promising a date on that call. The report most buyers want takes months of evidence, and nobody can hurry it.

So before you answer, find out what the buyer will actually accept and by when.

## What SOC 2 is

SOC 2 is a framework from the [AICPA](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/audit-assurance/audit-and-assurance-greater-than-soc-2), the American body that sets standards for accountants. A licensed CPA firm examines your controls, meaning the checks you say you run, and writes a report. Nobody gets certified. You get a document that describes your system, lists your controls and records what the auditor tested and found.

The auditor works from the AICPA's [Trust Services Criteria](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/resources/download/2017-trust-services-criteria-with-revised-points-of-focus-2022). Security is in every report. Availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy get added only when they matter to what you sell.

## Type 1 is quick, Type 2 takes months

A Type 1 report looks at your controls at one point in time and says whether they're designed properly.

A Type 2 report covers a period, often several months, and says whether the controls worked throughout it. If you say you review access every quarter, the auditor asks to see each review in that window, dated and signed off. Miss one and the report lists an exception.

Most enterprise buyers want Type 2. The controls have to be running before the window opens, and then the window has to run its course. You can't produce one by scrambling the week the auditor arrives.

## Where startups get caught

The most common mistake is starting too late. A deal hangs on the report, and the observation period hasn't started.

The second is copying a template policy set that describes a company you aren't. The auditor tests whether you do what your policies say, so every policy you don't follow becomes a finding. Write policies that match how your team really works.

The third is treating it as a one-off. Buyers usually want a current report every year, so the controls keep running after the first one.

If you sell mostly to consumers or small businesses and nobody has asked, a SOC 2 report is usually a cost with no buyer waiting for it. It also tends to come up in [technical due diligence](/blog/technical-due-diligence-explained), so expect investors to ask too.

## Separate the readiness work from the audit

A CPA firm issues the report. Everything before that is readiness: deciding the scope, finding the gaps, closing them and collecting evidence. Keep the scope tight, because everything inside it has to be documented and tested. Rank the gaps by how long they take to fix, since the slowest one sets your timeline. If you bring in help, ask who owns each part and what your own team still has to do. Our [SOC 2 readiness](/soc-2-readiness) page covers how we approach it.

## One thing to do this week

Ask the buyer which report type and scope they need, and by when. Compare that with the controls and evidence you already have. If the dates don't fit, say so early and ask what their security team can review in the meantime.
