Say a home-care agency buys a scheduling app. Two years later the coordinators still use it, but every morning they export a spreadsheet to check certifications, and they paste visit notes back by hand. The app works. It just doesn't cover the whole job anymore, and the workarounds never show up on an invoice.

Most businesses should buy most of their software. The decision only gets hard when a team is working around a tool every day, and then the comparison has to include that effort as well as the prices.

## Buying is usually right

Work that's much the same everywhere, like payroll, is best bought. The vendor has already met the edge cases you haven't, and it carries the cost of tax changes, security patches and new browser versions. A SaaS tool can be running in days. A custom system takes weeks at best, and a first version usually does less than the tool you could have bought. If the vendor's way of working is good enough, adopting it is cheaper than defending your own.

## The signs a tool no longer fits

Look for the work happening around the tool:

- A spreadsheet sits open beside it to do the part it can't.
- You pay for a higher tier, or a second tool, to fill one gap.
- The data for one decision lives in three places, and someone matches it up by hand.
- The vendor's roadmap decides what your team can do next.
- Customers pick you for the way you work, and the tool keeps pushing you toward the standard version.

One of these is normal. Three or more usually means the tool costs more than its invoice says.

## Compare the same period on both sides

A monthly subscription and a one-off build price cover different time spans and different responsibilities. On the buy side, add setup, data migration, training, the per-seat price as you hire, add-ons, renewal price rises, the hours spent on workarounds, and what it would cost to leave. On the build side, add the build, hosting and maintenance every year for as long as you use it, because the business keeps changing.

## Often the answer is a small addition

The choice is rarely all or nothing. A common answer is to keep the bought tools for the standard work and build a thin layer for the part that's specific to you, connected through the tools' APIs (the doors other software uses to read and write their data).

For the agency, that could mean keeping the scheduling app and building a small system for certifications and visit notes that reads from it and writes back to it. The morning export goes away, and nobody has to replace the parts of the app that work fine. Scope that addition the way you'd [scope an MVP](/blog/mvp-what-to-build-first): the smallest thing that removes the workaround.

It only works if the tools you buy have a decent API and let you get your data out. Check both before you sign, even if you have no plans to build anything yet.

## This week

Ask the people doing the work what they do by hand today and how many hours a week it takes. Price that per month, then compare it with a small addition or a replacement, including upkeep and who would look after it. If a vendor is on your shortlist, ask them to demonstrate your actual workflow, awkward exception included.
