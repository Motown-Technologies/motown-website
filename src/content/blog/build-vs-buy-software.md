Buy the SaaS tool when your process looks like everyone else's in your industry and the tool fits it without workarounds. Build custom software when the process is what sets you apart, or when your team spends more time working around the tool than working in it.

Most businesses should buy most of their software. Payroll, email, accounting and the help desk are solved problems, and a subscription costs less than anything we could build for you. The decision gets harder for the one or two systems at the centre of how you make money.

## When buying is the right call

Buy when the work is the same everywhere. Every company runs payroll in roughly the same way, and the vendor has already met the edge cases you haven't. The vendor also carries the cost of tax changes, security patches and new browser versions, which you would otherwise pay for yourself.

Buy when you need it next month. A SaaS tool can be running in days. A custom system takes weeks at best, and a first version usually does less than the tool you could have bought.

Buy when changing your process to match the tool would cost you nothing. If the vendor's way of doing it is good enough, adopting their workflow is cheaper than defending your own.

## Signs you've outgrown your SaaS tool

Say a home-care agency schedules visits in a well-known scheduling app. It worked for the first two years. Now the coordinators export the schedule to a spreadsheet every morning to check caregiver certifications, because the app has no field for them, and they paste visit notes back in by hand. Nobody decided to build a second system, but the agency has one, and it runs on copy and paste.

That pattern is common. These are the signs we look for:

- People keep a spreadsheet open beside the tool to do the part it can't.
- You pay for a higher tier, or a second tool, to fill one gap.
- The data you need for a decision lives in three places, and someone reconciles it by hand.
- The vendor's roadmap decides what your team can do next.
- Customers pick you for the way you work, and the tool keeps pushing you toward the standard version.

One of these is normal. Three or more usually means the tool costs more than its invoice says.

## Compare the total cost of building vs buying

A subscription looks cheap because it arrives as a small monthly number. A build looks expensive because it arrives as one large one. Comparing those two numbers tells you very little.

On the buy side, add the setup and data migration, the training, the per-seat price as you hire, the add-ons, the price rises at renewal, and the hours your team spends on workarounds. Then add what it would cost to leave: getting your data out in a usable form, and any contract terms that make leaving slow. When we run a [software selection](/software-selection-consulting), we model these over five years, and the shortlist often reorders once exit costs are in.

On the build side, add the build itself, hosting and ongoing maintenance. Custom software needs looking after for as long as you use it. Dependencies need updating and the business keeps changing, so budget for that every year.

## The middle path: buy the standard parts, build your own

The choice is rarely all or nothing. A common answer is to buy the tools that handle standard work and build a thin layer for the part that is specific to you, connected to the bought tools through their APIs.

In the home-care example, that could mean keeping the scheduling app and building a small system for certifications and visit notes that reads from it and writes back to it. The coordinators stop exporting spreadsheets, and nobody has to replace the parts of the app that work fine.

This only works if the tools you buy have a decent API and let you get your data out. Check both before you sign, even if you have no plans to build anything yet.

## What we ask before recommending either

When a client brings us this decision, we ask four questions before anyone writes code:

1. Which part of this process do your customers actually notice?
2. What are people doing by hand today, and how many hours a week does it take?
3. Does the tool you have, or one you could buy, let you read and write your data through an API?
4. Who will own and look after the software once it's built?

If the answers point to buying, we say so. A careful selection with scripted demos and a contract review costs less than a build, and it often solves the problem. If they point to building, we scope the smallest version that removes the workaround, the same way we decide [what goes into an MVP](/blog/mvp-what-to-build-first).

## How to decide

Buy for the work that's the same everywhere. Build for the work that makes you different, or that your team already rebuilds by hand every day. When it's unclear, write down what the workarounds cost each month. That figure usually settles the question.
