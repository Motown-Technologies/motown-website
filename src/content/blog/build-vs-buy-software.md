Say a home-care agency buys a scheduling app. Two years later, the coordinators still use it, but they also export a spreadsheet every morning to check certifications and paste visit notes back by hand. The app still works, but it no longer covers the whole job.

Most businesses should buy most of their software. When a team is working around a tool every day, though, the comparison needs to include that effort as well as the subscription and development prices.

## When buying is the right call

Buying makes sense for work that is much the same across businesses. Every company runs payroll in roughly the same way, and the vendor has already met the edge cases you haven't. The vendor also carries the cost of tax changes, security patches and new browser versions, which you would otherwise pay for yourself.

A short deadline also favors buying. A SaaS tool can be running in days. A custom system takes weeks at best, and a first version usually does less than the tool you could have bought.

Consider how much it would cost to adapt your process to the tool. If the vendor's way of doing it is good enough, adopting their workflow is cheaper than defending your own.

## Signs you've outgrown your SaaS tool

In the home-care example, a spreadsheet has become an unofficial second system. To work out whether yours has too, look for the work around the tool:

- People keep a spreadsheet open beside the tool to do the part it can't.
- You pay for a higher tier, or a second tool, to fill one gap.
- The data you need for a decision lives in three places, and someone reconciles it by hand.
- The vendor's roadmap decides what your team can do next.
- Customers pick you for the way you work, and the tool keeps pushing you toward the standard version.

One of these is normal. Three or more usually means the tool costs more than its invoice says.

## Compare the total cost of building vs buying

A monthly subscription and a one-off build price cover different periods and responsibilities. Compare the costs over the same period before deciding which is cheaper.

On the buy side, add the setup and data migration, the training, the per-seat price as you hire, the add-ons, the price rises at renewal, and the hours your team spends on workarounds. Then add what it would cost to leave: getting your data out in a usable form, and any contract terms that make leaving slow.

On the build side, add the build itself, hosting and ongoing maintenance. Custom software needs looking after for as long as you use it. Dependencies need updating and the business keeps changing, so budget for that every year.

## The middle path: buy the standard parts, build your own

The choice is rarely all or nothing. A common answer is to buy the tools that handle standard work and build a thin layer for the part that is specific to you, connected to the bought tools through their APIs.

In the home-care example, that could mean keeping the scheduling app and building a small system for certifications and visit notes that reads from it and writes back to it. The coordinators stop exporting spreadsheets, and nobody has to replace the parts of the app that work fine.

This only works if the tools you buy have a decent API and let you get your data out. Check both before you sign, even if you have no plans to build anything yet.

## Questions to answer with the people doing the work

Before anyone writes code, work through these questions with the team:

1. Which part of this process do your customers actually notice?
2. What are people doing by hand today, and how many hours a week does it take?
3. Does the tool you have, or one you could buy, let you read and write your data through an API?
4. Who will own and look after the software once it's built?

Ask shortlisted vendors to demonstrate your actual workflow, including the awkward exception. If the existing tools still cannot cover it, scope the smallest addition that removes the workaround. The same discipline helps decide [what goes into an MVP](/blog/mvp-what-to-build-first).

## How to decide

For the parts that remain unclear, work out what the workarounds cost each month. Compare that with the cost of a small addition or a replacement, including maintenance and who would take responsibility for it.
