An investor asks who owns the code. The founder knows the contractor was paid, but isn't sure where the signed assignment is. Another question asks what the platform will cost at ten times the load. Nobody has modeled it yet.

Technical due diligence brings questions like these into an investment or acquisition. Preparing means finding the evidence, identifying the gaps and deciding how to address or disclose them before the deal depends on an answer.

## What the reviewer will ask about

### Architecture

The reviewer looks at whether the system can support the next 18 months of the roadmap, including any plans that depend on an unbudgeted rewrite.

### Code quality

The reviewer examines test coverage in the places it matters and how consistent the code is. The practical test is how much of the codebase a new engineer could safely change in their first month.

### Security

Expect questions about sign-in, access control, stored secrets, exposed data and dependencies with known vulnerabilities. Gather the evidence behind your answers, including issues the team already knows need work.

### Infrastructure and cost

Bring the current running costs and a model of what they would look like at ten times the load. Identify the assumptions, especially line items whose growth nobody has measured.

### Team and key-person risk

Some systems are understood by only one person. Identify those, note what is undocumented, and ask what would happen if that person left during the process. For a view of a handover from the receiving end, we wrote about [inheriting a codebase that still works](/blog/inherited-codebase).

### IP and licensing

Find the signed agreements assigning contractor-built code to the company and an inventory of dependency licenses. Paying an invoice does not answer every ownership question, and the reviewer needs evidence that can be checked.

## What a useful finding should tell you

A finding should explain the problem in plain language and show the evidence. It also needs enough context to support a decision: what could happen, how likely that is and what a repair would involve.

Separate work needed before the deal from work that can be planned afterward. Ask for an owner and an estimated cost, with uncertainty made clear. That lets the founder, engineering team and investor discuss the same problem in the same terms.

Keep a short summary for the data room, with the detailed findings and cost model available behind it. The summary should make the unresolved questions visible.

## Leave time to respond

A known problem can be investigated, fixed or disclosed with a plan. A problem first raised late in the deal gives both sides less time to understand it and can affect the negotiation.

Allow time before opening the data room to gather records and work through gaps. The right lead time depends on what is missing. Finding a signed agreement will need a different schedule from redesigning a system.

Ask the team to assemble the evidence it already has before ordering a new report. Missing documents and unanswered ownership questions deserve attention even if no deal is imminent. For an approaching raise, prepare a prioritized list of known gaps with the evidence already available.
