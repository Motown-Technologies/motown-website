An investor asks who owns the code. The founder knows the contractor was paid but isn't sure where the signed assignment is. The next question is what the platform will cost at ten times the load, and nobody has modeled it.

That's technical due diligence, the part of an investment or acquisition where someone checks the software. The best preparation is to find your evidence and your gaps before the reviewer does, then decide which gaps to fix and which to disclose.

## What the reviewer looks at

The questions usually cover these areas:

- Whether the architecture can carry the next 18 months of the roadmap, and any plan that depends on an unbudgeted rewrite.
- How much of the codebase a new engineer could safely change in their first month.
- Sign-in, access control, stored secrets, exposed data and outside code with known vulnerabilities.
- Running costs today and a model at ten times the load, with the assumptions marked, especially line items whose growth nobody has measured.
- Systems only one person understands, and what happens if that person leaves mid-deal.
- Who owns the code, and a list of licenses for the outside code you use.

On ownership, paying an invoice doesn't settle every question. The reviewer wants signed agreements assigning contractor-built code to the company, something they can check.

## A known problem is easier than a late one

It's tempting to hope a weak spot goes unnoticed. We'd raise it first. A problem you already know about can be investigated, fixed or disclosed with a plan. One that surfaces late in the deal leaves both sides less time to understand it and can affect the negotiation.

The cost is time. You need room before the data room opens to gather records and work through gaps, and how much depends on what's missing. Tracking down a signed agreement runs on a different schedule from redesigning a system.

A useful finding explains the problem in plain language and shows the evidence. It says what could happen, how likely that is and what a repair would involve. It separates work needed before the deal from work that can be planned afterward, and comes with an owner and an estimated cost, uncertainty included. Then the founder, the engineers and the investor are discussing the same problem in the same terms.

## One thing to do this week

Find the signed IP assignment for every contractor who has written code for you. If any are missing, start there, even with no deal on the horizon. Then ask your team to pull together the evidence they already have before anyone orders a new report.
