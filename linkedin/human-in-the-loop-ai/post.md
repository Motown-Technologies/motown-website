## Post 1: native text

Putting a person in front of an AI's work only helps if that person can keep up.

An AI drafts, a person approves. Sounds safe, until they've got a hundred near-identical items and a full day of other work. And AI sounds just as confident when it's wrong, so they can't judge a draft by its tone.

First decide where the person goes. Ask what happens if this output is wrong and nobody checks. "Someone rewrites a draft" is cheap. "A refund goes out" means a person sees it first. Deleting data or changing permissions stays outside the AI's reach entirely.

Then make the review screen something a busy person can use. Put the photo, document or customer's message beside the draft. Make editing as easy as approving. A refund twice the usual size should look different from the rest.

At an auction house, we built a system where an AI reads item photos and drafts the catalog title, description and keywords. A cataloger reviews each one, asks for another try when it's off, and approves it into inventory. The AI never publishes on its own.

Start with more review than you need. After a few weeks the approval records show what people almost never change, and that can move to spot checks.
