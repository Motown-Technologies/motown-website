# LinkedIn: when-a-spreadsheet-should-become-a-database

## Post 1: native text

If only one person understands your spreadsheet, their vacation is already a planning problem.

They know which tab is current and why a number looks off. When something breaks, everyone waits. That alone is a reason to think about a database, even for a small workbook. Size usually isn't: heavy formulas slow most business sheets down long before they run out of rows.

The other signs tend to arrive together. The same customer appears in three tabs and the tabs don't agree. More than one file has "final" in its name. Nobody can say who changed a number, or when.

An auction house we worked with kept item status in a spreadsheet, a shared photo folder and a group chat. An item could be "photographed" in the sheet and "still in storage" in the chat. In the system we built, each item's status follows what has happened to it, so nobody types updates by hand and every screen gives the same answer.

A spreadsheet is still the right tool when one person owns it, when it's for analysis, or when the process behind it changes every week.

Before you replace one, ask whoever knows it to walk you through a normal day and a hard one, and write down the exceptions. The article covers the rest of the move. Link's in the comments.
